## Part 1: Affinity Diagramming

### What is Affinity Diagramming?

> A process in which we revise collected data and organise it **by affinity** to identify key problems and areas for design intervention.

- Primary and secondary user research generates large amounts of data that can be hard to process
- Affinity diagramming is a structured way to make sense of that data
- Referenced resource: Norman Nielsen Group's affinity diagramming practice

### Key Characteristics

- Best done **collaboratively** (in a team)
- Uses physical or digital post-it notes on a large shared space
- Focuses specifically on **personal experiential information** — what users _do_, _think_, and _feel_
- Organises information under **relevant clusters and categories** related to the design brief
- Assigns **headers** to categories, which leads to identification and definition of **problem areas**

### Tools for Affinity Diagramming

**Physical:**

- Post-it notes and markers on a whiteboard or wall

**Digital:**

- **Miro** — must access with UTS credentials
- **Mural**
- **FigJam**

### The 4-Step Affinity Diagramming Process

#### Step 1 — Extract Findings (Individually)

- Each team member reviews their own data
- Extract keywords, short sentences, and/or quotes
- Write one finding per post-it / Miro note

#### Step 2 — Display Findings (As a Team)

- Place all notes on the shared team space **in random order**
- Get the "greater picture" before organising

#### Step 3 — Sort Findings into Themes

- Group related findings by affinity (into clusters or columns)
- Establish themes for each group
- Repeat until all findings are under a theme
- Duplicate notes if a finding fits multiple themes
- Leave "loners" (unclassifiable findings) in a separate column

#### Step 4 — Create Headers for Themes

- Headers = labels that **capture the essence** of each theme
- Headers should be a word or short phrase
- Should be **clear enough for someone outside the team** to understand
- How to develop headers:
    - Discuss and agree on an appropriate label
    - Rename an existing theme group
    - Find an existing note that serves as the header
    - Discover relationships between 2+ groups → create a **super-header**

### Structure of an Affinity Diagram

```
| Header/Topic 1 | Header/Topic 2 | Header/Topic 3 | Header/Topic 4 |
|----------------|----------------|----------------|----------------|
| [Blue note]    | [Blue note]    | [Blue note]    | [Blue note]    |  ← Affinity Labels
|                |                |                |                |
| [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  |  ← Affinity Notes
| [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  |
| [Yellow note]  |                | [Yellow note]  | [Yellow note]  |
```

**Affinity Labels (headers):**

- Group common themes
- Have design relevance
- Written in the _voice of the user_

**Affinity Notes (findings):**

- Issues and needs
- Emerge directly from data

### DOs and DON'Ts for Affinity Notes

|DON'T|DO|
|---|---|
|Start from pre-conceived categories|Move notes around; avoid pre-conceived categories|
|Worry about placing a note in the "right" place|Create new distinctions by moving notes — the labels matter, not individual data points|
|Find a "keyword" and assume it belongs in that category|Read the full note to find the **underlying implication**|
|Take too long in the initial placement step|Get notes into rough groupings quickly; refine later|

> **Example:** All notes with the word "printing" do **not** automatically go into a column about printing — read for the underlying meaning.

### DOs and DON'Ts for Affinity Labels/Headers

|DON'T|DO|
|---|---|
|Write labels in the third person|Write as though **the user is speaking** — use "I" and "We"|
|Write labels that are too abstract|Capture what the group is saying in detail|
|Use your own jargon|Write clear labels that everyone can understand|
|Use pre-defined categories|Let the data tell the story|
|Force a label on a group|Reorganise the group if a good label can't be found|
|Bury potentially important distinctions|Surface important design points in labels|

_Source: Goodwin, K. (2011). Designing for the Digital Age. John Wiley & Sons._

## Part 2: Representing Users in the Design Process

### Why Do We Represent Users?

- To initially **understand the problem space**
- To act upon it and **improve** our understanding of it
- Because it is **not practical or feasible** to involve users themselves in the design process for extended periods
- To help the team and stakeholders **focus on users** throughout design
- To **build empathy** for users within the design team
- To **communicate** who the users are to others
- To **validate and prioritise** design decisions and actions

### What Do We Need to Represent?

- Relevant information and ideas
- A **variety** of users and other stakeholders
- Different forms of use, contexts, and situations

### Limitations of Representations

All user representations are:

- **Translations** — they differ from actual entities
- **Partial** — they include some facts while excluding others
- **Provisional** — they evolve during the design process
- **Multiple** — there are many ways to represent users

### Representation vs. Direct Presentation

|Type|Example|Classification|
|---|---|---|
|Photo|Actual photograph of a person|NOT a representation (direct presentation)|
|Avatar|Illustrated character|A representation|
|Icon|Generic silhouette|A representation|

> **Key insight:** "You are not your user" — you are designing for people unlike yourself. Research and understand your actual user.

### Design Representations and Artefacts

Design artefacts support the design process by helping to **maintain context** over time, between meetings, and among different team members.

Types of design artefacts include:

- User research data
- Documents and briefs
- User journeys
- **Personas**
- Scenarios
- Diagrams
- Prototypes
- Plans

### FID Design Artefacts (used in this course)

The following are used across AT3:

1. **Persona** ← focus of this lecture
2. Problem scenario
3. Future use scenario
4. Storyboard
5. Prototype
6. Usability report
7. Design-in-action video

> These representations become more useful and important as systems become more complex — they act as **props to manage complexity** without needing to deal with full complexity at all times.

## Part 3: Personas

### Definition

> **Personas** are fictional representations and generalisations in the form of characters created by designers **based on research insights**.

### Key Characteristics

- A **narrative description** of a user's background, characteristics, motivations, goals, and needs
- **Composites of a diversity of real people** — not of a single person
- Not real people but **realistic (human-like)** — an aggregation of personalities based on actual behavioural patterns observed **through research**
- **Not generalisations** (too vague) **or stereotypes** (too biased)

### What Personas Represent

Personas capture meaningful commonalities across user groups, including:

- **Motivations** (re: life, work, relationships)
- **User needs and goals** (re: products)
- **Attitudes and preferences**
- **Frustrations and fears**
- **Levels of technology use** (familiarity)
- **Contexts and experiences**

They are:

- **Problem-focused** — help understand the user AND the problem space
- **Solution-oriented** — inform design decisions
- **Multiple** — more than one persona is created per project, as not all users are the same

### The Persona–Designer Relationship

- Personas are one of the **most commonly used** design artefacts in IxD/UX
- Should be a **vivid and engaging reference** for the design team
- Keep users "alive" during the design process
- Only as good as the **research behind them**
- Help designers understand users' needs, experiences, behaviours, and goals

### Personas and Real Data

|Element|Real?|
|---|---|
|Personal information|✗ — fictional composite|
|Photo|✗ — must not be real interviewee photo|
|Bio|✗ — constructed from research patterns|
|Family and friends|✗ — illustrative only|
|Motivations|✗ — synthesised from research|
|Goals and needs|✗ — synthesised from research|
|Relationship with products|✗ — synthesised from research|
|**Research insights**|**✓ — MUST be based on real data**|

> **Critical point:** A persona IS NOT a summary of a single person you interviewed. It IS a synthesis/composite of multiple people you interviewed.

### The Road to Persona — Construction Steps

```
Step 1: Interview Data
        ↓
  Interview 1 + Interview 2 + Interview 3 + ... Interview N

Step 2: Affinity Diagram
        ↓
  Look for common themes / problems / needs / desires
  → Affinity Labels (headers)
  → Affinity Notes (findings)

Step 3: Personas
        ↓
  Based on common themes found in affinity diagram
  → NOT a summary of a single interview or person
  → DOES include details from multiple interviews sharing common characteristics
  → CAN include specific details from individual interviews (e.g. technologies used, habits)
```

> You _can_ include a few specific details from individual interviews, but don't create a persona as a summary of a single interview.

## Good Persona vs. Bad Persona

### What Makes a GOOD Persona (Example: "Ginnie")

- ✅ Quick list of background info
- ✅ Specific personal details and preferences
- ✅ One-sentence summary statement / personal quote
- ✅ Some pictures to enliven the descriptions
- ✅ Credible, realistic name
- ✅ Motivations, frustrations clearly listed

### What Makes a BAD Persona (Example: "Clark Andrews")

- ❌ Motivations **not specific enough** (vague bar chart labels like "Incentive", "Fear")
- ❌ **Use scales on visualisations** — bars shown without labels or values
- ❌ Similar kinds of information **scattered** across the layout (not well organised)
- ❌ Generic, random content unconnected to actual user research
- ❌ Non-realistic image (AI art / cartoon) — does not elicit empathy

### What Makes a BAD Persona (Example: "Fungus Winterbottom")

- ❌ **Unrealistic name** — breaks immersion, reduces empathy
- ❌ Bio is **too text heavy** — hard to scan and reference
- ❌ **Wasted space** — large empty areas
- ❌ Personal quote **too small** to be prominent
- ❌ Motivations have **no scale or detail** — too non-specific

## What to Include in Your Persona

### Core Sections (Required for AT3)

|Section|What to Include|
|---|---|
|**Profile information**|Age, gender, marital status, children, occupation, expertise|
|**User problem**|Personal quote (in the user's voice) OR a specific problem statement|
|**Bio / Day in the life**|Short biography OR detailed "a day in the life" narrative|
|**Goals and needs**|Regarding wellbeing at home (relevant to design brief)|
|**Images**|Personal choices — activities, objects, tools, products, technologies|
|**Delights**|Joys, motivations, hopes, desires|
|**Pains**|Fears, frustrations|
|**Character**|Personality and behaviour|
|**Habits**|Routines, hobbies, activities|
|**Life at home**|Home environment|
|**Technology**|Technology use, literacy, preferences|

> **Important:** Content must be derived from your and your team members' interview summaries, observations, and data analysis (affinity diagramming).

### Practical Guidelines

- Bring personas to life with a **credible name**, characteristics, goals, personal background, and relationships
- Include a **brief bio**: jobs, hobbies, interests, type of person
- Show attitude or **relationship with interactive technologies**
- **Don't mirror** one real person
- **Don't "average"** your different user characteristics
- **Don't idealise** them either
- Address **needs, wants, hopes, wishes** surrounding the problem
- Address **challenges and obstacles** surrounding the problem

## AT3 Persona Rules (Instructor Emphasis)

### Must Have

- ✅ A **problem statement** or **user quote** — because personas are _problem-focused_

### Must NOT Have

- ❌ **Real photo of the interviewee** — personas are composites; we protect participant identities
- ❌ **Photos of celebrities** — breaks realism and empathy
- ❌ **Cartoons or animated characters** — not human-like enough
- ❌ **AI-generated fantasy/stylised art** — does not elicit empathy
- ✅ **Use a realistic stock photo** of a stranger that fits the persona's profile
