## Part 1 — Immersion & Engagement

### Immersion

Two meanings of the word (both relevant to IxD):

- **Literal (physical):** The act of putting something completely under the surface of a liquid
- **Experiential (relevant to design):** The fact of becoming completely involved in something — losing all sense of space and time

> *"This game is so deeply immersive that you lose all sense of space and time!"*

#### Immersion in Media Contexts

Cinema provides a useful spectrum to understand different *levels* of immersion:

- **Standard cinema** — passive, screen-based; still quite immersive through story and sound
- **IMAX** — larger screen, enhanced speaker arrays (surround sound), greater visual field
- **ICE (Immersive Cinema Experience)** — side LED panels extend the visual field beyond the front screen
- **ScreenX** — main + side screens wrap around the audience
- **4DX** — adds physical effects (water spray, seat movement, scent); multi-sensory

Other immersive formats beyond cinema:
- **Planetaria** — dome projection surrounds audience with sky simulation
- **Immersive exhibitions** (e.g. teamLab Borderless, Tokyo) — interactive, spatial, audiovisual environments
- **Theatres** — spatial staging, live performance

#### Immersion in Games

Games offer immersion across a wide spectrum of modalities and interaction types.

**Modes and Modalities to consider:**
- Visual
- Auditory
- Tangible (physical touch/haptics)
- Gustatory (taste — rare but exists)
- Olfactory (smell — rare but exists)

**Interaction Styles/Types:**
- Instructing
- Conversing
- Manipulating
- Exploring

**Examples across immersion levels:**

| Format | Description |
|---|---|
| Traditional screen + controller | PC/console gaming; visual + auditory; instructing/manipulating |
| VR headset + touch controllers | First-person visual immersion; physical interaction |
| VR Escape Room (e.g. The Cave, NZ) | Collaborative, spatial, multi-sensory |
| Hybrid mediascape room (e.g. Farbound, Sky Princess cruise) | Physical props + digital environment overlay |

### Engagement

#### General Definition

- **Being involved with or in something**
- **Online context:** The process of people looking at and reacting to particular information online (e.g. content such as videos and memes improving social media engagement)

*(Source: Cambridge Dictionary)*

#### IxD Definition

> In Interaction Design, engagement is the **level of emotional, cognitive and physical interaction and involvement** that users have with a product.

- It is a **key metric** for measuring the success of interactive design

#### High vs Low Engagement

| Engagement Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **High** | Users find the design compelling, enjoyable, and valuable |
| **Low** | There are issues with the design that need to be addressed |

---

### Immersion vs Engagement — Key Distinction

| Concept | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Immersion** | The *feeling* of being inside another environment; being surrounded by the elements/entities of another space, context, or environment |
| **Engagement** | The *state* in which one is attracted to the application, its features, and content; how much absorption, enjoyment, and value a user gets from a product |

## Part 2 — Usability Evaluation

### What is Usability?

#### Definition 1 — Interaction Design Foundation

> "A measure of how well a specific user in a specific context can **use a product/design to achieve a defined goal effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily.**"

#### Definition 2 — ISO 9241 (Ergonomics of Human System Interaction)

> "The extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specific goals with **effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction** in a specified context of use."

**Three components (ISO):**

- **Effectiveness** — "the accuracy and completeness with which users can achieve goals in particular environments"
- **Efficiency** — "the resources expended in relation to the accuracy and completeness of the goals achieved"
- **Satisfaction** — "the comfort and acceptability of the work system to its users and other people affected by its use"

#### Position in the UX Hierarchy

- Usability is the **second level** in user experience
  - After: **Utility** (practical — allows goal achievement)
  - Before: **Desirability**
- **Useful** = practical, allowing the achievement of a goal in an effective way
- **Usable** = pleasurable, both the process towards *and* reaching of the goal are enjoyed
- Usability is measured **throughout the whole development process** — from wireframes to the final deliverable

---

### The 5 Es of Usability

A framework describing the five dimensions of usability:

| E | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Effective** | How completely and accurately the work or experience is completed, or goals are reached |
| **Efficient** | How quickly this work can be completed with optimal use of resources |
| **Engaging** | How well the interface draws the user into the interaction and how pleasant and satisfying it is to use |
| **Error Tolerant** | How well the product prevents errors and can help the user recover from mistakes that do occur |
| **Easy to Learn** | How well the product supports both the initial orientation and continued learning throughout the complete lifetime of use |

---

### What is User Experience?

#### Definition — ISO 9241

> "A person's **perceptions and responses** that result from the use or anticipated use of a product, system or service."

- UX includes all users' **emotions, beliefs, preferences, perceptions, physical and psychological responses, behaviours and accomplishments** that occur **before, during and after use**
- Usability is measured within the overall UX at the **times and places of direct contact** with the product

#### Relationship: Usability → UX → CX
[ Usability ]
— user-friendliness during use
[ User Experience ]
— perception of and reactions to usability, information structure,
interaction design, and visual design before, during, and after use
[ Customer Experience ]
— all-encompassing customer experiences at multiple online and
offline touchpoints along the entire customer journey

*(Nested rings: Usability ⊂ UX ⊂ CX)*

---

### Design vs Use

> **Key insight:** We design with the user in mind, but unless we **test our design with our users**, we won't know if we've been successful.

- Design is an **iterative process** — we continue to refine based on insights from users we test with
- The "desire path" analogy: users will follow the most natural route regardless of what the designer intended

---

### Usability Evaluation

#### Why Evaluate?

**During Development:**
- Predict the usability of a product or an aspect of it
- Check the design team's understanding of the requirements
- Test out ideas quickly and informally

**Post-Production:**
- Identify user difficulties
- Fine-tune the product to its context of use
- Improve an upgrade of a product
- May start as a 'public beta' version

> Whilst many of the evaluation methods remain the same, the **reasons why they're used are slightly different** at each stage.

### Common Usability Problems

Indicators that usability issues exist during testing:

- Behaviours that **prevent task completion**
- An expression of **frustration** by the participant
- **Not seeing** something that should be noticed
- A participant says a task is **complete when it is not**
- Performing an action that **leads away from task success**
- **Misinterpreting** some piece of content
- Choosing the **wrong link** to navigate through screens

---

### Usability Evaluation / Testing Methods

> Tests can be done in isolation or in combination with other types of tests to get a rich picture and valuable insights. There are different ways to document tests and synthesise data.

---

#### Questionnaires

Standardised instruments for gathering usability data.

**System Usability Scale (SUS)**
- Source: Brooke, J. (1986). "SUS: a 'quick and dirty' usability scale"
- 10 Likert-scale statements (Strongly Disagree → Strongly Agree)
- Example items:
  - "I think that I would like to use this website frequently"
  - "I found this website unnecessarily complex"
  - "I thought this website was easy to use"
  - "I found the various functions in this website were well integrated"
  - "I felt very confident using this website"

**AttrakDiff**
- Source: [attrakdiff.de](http://www.attrakdiff.de/)
- Uses **word pairs** (semantic differential scale) to capture hedonic and pragmatic quality
- Example pairs: human/technical, isolating/connective, pleasant/unpleasant, inventive/conventional, simple/complicated, professional/unprofessional, ugly/attractive, practical/impractical, likeable/disagreeable, cumbersome/straightforward

**Other Questionnaire Tools:**
- **SUMI** — Software Usability Measurement Inventory
- **PSSUQ** — Post-Study Usability Questionnaire
- **QUIS** — Questionnaire for User Interaction Satisfaction (50 statements)

---

#### Observation

A common method involving:
1. **Providing a task** to the user (e.g. "Transfer $100 from your savings to your daily account")
2. **Observing** the user whilst they attempt to complete the task

**Key notes:**
- Observation can tell us *what* somebody did, but **not necessarily why**
- Best combined with interviews to ask beyond what is observed
- Can be filmed, written, or both
- Structure notes into sections relating to **interaction sequences** of the process
- The act of observing can **affect the outcome** (observer effect — they may behave differently)
- Include **metadata**: context (e.g. noisy environment, performance pressure) to make sense of observations later

**Settings for Observation:**

| Setting | Description |
|---|---|
| **Usability Lab** | Controlled environment, often with one-way mirror; allows separate observer room + recording equipment |
| **In User's Context** | Observe in the user's natural environment (e.g. home, workplace); more ecological validity |

> The Norwegian film **Kitchen Stories (2003)** is referenced as an example of ethnographic observation in context — a researcher observing a subject in their kitchen.

---

#### The Think-Aloud Method

- A way to understand a person's **cognitive process**
- Rather than asking a person to *recall* how they do something, they are asked to **'think aloud' as they complete the process**

**Example:**
- ❌ "Tell me how you transfer money between accounts" (retrospective recall)
- ✅ Ask the person to **explain their actions and reasoning *during* the process** of transferring money

**Advantages:**
- Gets **inside the head of the user** — captures thinking *and* feeling during interaction
- Particularly useful **combined with observation**
- Surfaces issues invisible to observers alone (e.g. "I'm struggling to find where to change the amount I just entered…")

**Limitations:**
- A user may say what they think **you want to hear**, rather than what they actually think

---

#### Debrief Interview Method

- Like a **semi-structured interview** (open and closed-ended questions) on the specific topic: testing of X to achieve Y
- Conducted **immediately after the test** — particular details may fade from memory quickly, and recollections of usability problems may be altered
- The interviewer asks questions about what was **observed or spoken about** during the usability test
- Allows the user to **reflect** on their experience and give a more considered account
- The interviewer may **prompt** with questions, examples (photos, transcribed comments), or objects (prototype samples, related products)

**Limitation:**
- Interviewees may report a **'false' reflection**, embarrassed by encountering usability issues

---

#### Heuristic Evaluation

- Conducted by a **small group of reviewers/experts** (e.g. designers) — *not* target users
- Experts examine the interface and judge its compliance with recognised **usability principles (heuristics)**
- The outcome is a list of **critical issues, observations and recommendations** consolidated into a report

**Process:**
1. Provide heuristics to the experts
2. Experts go through the interface(s) **twice**: first to understand, then to assess against heuristics
3. Expert analysis
4. Discussion of results with the experts
5. Experts provide **severity rating / report**

**Severity Rating Scale (Nielsen):**

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **0** | Don't agree that this is a usability problem |
| **1** | Cosmetic problem |
| **2** | Minor usability problem |
| **3** | Major usability problem; important to fix |
| **4** | Usability catastrophe; imperative to fix |

**Effectiveness of multiple evaluators:**
- An average **single evaluator finds ~35%** of issues
- **5 evaluators can find ~75%** of issues between them

---

#### Other Methods

- **Behaviour Tracking** (e.g. Google Analytics) — tracks user navigation, time-on-page, drop-off points, etc.
- **Eye Tracking** — records where users look on screen; produces heat maps and gaze paths; reveals what is and isn't noticed
- **Cognitive Load / Bio Tracking** — physiological sensors (e.g. wearable patches) measure stress, cognitive load, arousal during interaction

---

### Summary Table — Evaluation Methods and Output Data

| Method | Output Data |
|---|---|
| **User observation** | Verbal protocol (think-aloud or retrospective); performance times; log of errors; satisfaction ratings |
| **Cognitive Walkthrough** | Verbal protocol of user's mental processes *(evaluates whether the order of cues and prompts supports the way people process tasks and anticipate "next steps")* |
| **Heuristic Inspection** | List of usability defects with severity ratings |
| **Mixed** (interviews, anecdotes, system/error logs) | Participant comments; evaluator comments/hints/notes; details of interface/interaction; participant behaviour; unusual/unexpected events; breakdowns/interventions |

## Usability Heuristics Review (Week 2 Reminder)

### Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics (2006)

> Broad "rules of thumb", originally created in 2001 as general principles for UI design. Now used mostly as an **evaluation framework**.
>
> Full updated heuristics: [nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics)

---

#### 1. Visibility of System Status

> Users should always be informed of system operations with **easy to understand and highly visible status** displayed on the screen within a **reasonable amount of time**.

**Examples:**
- Multi-step form showing progress indicators (Saved → Submitted → Criteria Checked → Approved)
- macOS install progress bar with time remaining
- File upload showing "UPLOADING 3 OF 3 FILES" with individual file progress
- Shopping cart showing "1 in cart" badge
- Stock availability indicator ("ONLY A FEW LEFT")

---

#### 2. Match Between the System and the Real World

> Designers should **mirror the language and concepts users would find in the real world** based on who their target users are.

**Example:**
- A compass app whose UI elements (directional letters, circular dial, degree markings) visually mirror a physical compass — users can immediately understand its function

---

#### 3. User Control and Freedom

> Offer users a **digital space where backward steps are possible**, including undoing and redoing previous actions.

**Examples:**
- Adobe Photoshop Edit menu → Undo / Step Backward (Ctrl+Z / Alt+Ctrl+Z)
- Gmail: cancel file attachment mid-upload; "Undo" option after sending a message
- Browser back/forward navigation arrows

---

#### 4. Consistency and Standards

> Interface designers should ensure that both **graphic elements and terminology are maintained across similar platforms**.

**Example:**
- Amazon's website and mobile app share consistent layout, colour palette, icons, navigation labels, and terminology — users don't need to relearn when switching between platforms

---

#### 5. Error Prevention

> A careful design which **prevents a problem from occurring** in the first place. Either eliminate error-prone conditions or check for them and **present users with a confirmation option** before they commit to the action.

**Examples:**
- Password creation field with live strength indicator and requirement checklist (prevents weak password submission)
- Chrome "Leave site? Changes that you made may not be saved" confirmation dialog

---

#### 6. Recognition Rather Than Recall

> **Minimise cognitive load** by maintaining task-relevant information within the display while users explore the interface. Human attention is limited — we can hold approximately **~5 items in short-term memory** at once.

**Example:**
- Photoshop home screen shows **visual thumbnails** of recent files so users recognise their work rather than needing to remember file names

---

#### 7. Flexibility and Efficiency of Use

> Faster navigation can be achieved via abbreviations, function keys, hidden commands, and macro facilities. Users should be able to **customise or tailor the interface** to suit their needs so that frequent actions can be achieved more conveniently.

**Example:**
- Microsoft Word shortcut menus and keyboard shortcuts (Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V, etc.) allow expert users to bypass menu navigation

---

#### 8. Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

> The display must be **reduced to only the necessary components for the current task**, whilst providing clearly visible and unambiguous means of navigating to other content.

**Example:**
- A weather app redesign: removing the multi-row forecast from the main view and replacing it with a full-bleed city image + essential current conditions — less clutter, cleaner hierarchy

---

#### 9. Help Users Recognise, Diagnose and Recover from Errors

> Error messages should be expressed in **plain language** (no technical terminology) — indicate what the problem is and suggest a solution.

**Examples:**
- YouTube offline screen: "Connect to the Internet / You're offline. Check your connection." — friendly illustration + plain instruction
- Pinterest login: "The email you entered does not belong to any account." — specific, actionable error message

---

#### 10. Help and Documentation

> Even if the interface can be used without documentation, it may be necessary to provide help. Any help information should be **easy to search, focused on the user's task, list concrete steps, and not be too large**.

**Example:**
- Contextual tooltip on a form field (e.g. clicking a "?" icon reveals a short explanation of the required input format with concrete examples)

**Five columns to complete for each Screen/State:**

| Column | What to Include |
|---|---|
| **Usability Assessment** | Summarise positive AND negative finding(s) observed; include page/screen where finding was observed; the specific part of the task the user was doing when it occurred |
| **Identification of Heuristics** | For each finding, specify which heuristic(s) were **Violated (V)** or **Complied with (C)**; refer to the list above |
| **Explanation of Heuristics** | Explain HOW the heuristics are violated or complied with; indicate the *cause*; refer to specific design aspects of the interface — **NOT** just what the heuristic is |
| **Usability Impact** | Explain how the observed findings impacted the user experience and task completion |
| **Usability Suggestion(s)** | Suggest at least one interface design intervention per screen to rectify the issue or improve the experience |
